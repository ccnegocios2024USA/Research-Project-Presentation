Section 2

History of Migration and Its Causes

Research on the movement of the Guerrero community to Washington State.

Why the Yakima Valley?

The Yakima Valley became the primary destination due to its massive agricultural industry. Unlike large industrial cities, Yakima offered a rural, family-oriented environment very similar to the Mexican countryside.

The constant demand for skilled labor in agriculture served as the definitive bridge connecting towns in southern Mexico with the Pacific Northwest of the United States.

Push Factors

Rural Economic Crisis

The collapse of commercial prices for staple crops like corn and coffee in Guerrero left thousands of peasant families without basic livelihoods during the late 20th century.

Environmental Challenges

Prolonged droughts, lack of irrigation infrastructure, and severe soil erosion in the "La Montaña" region made subsistence farming nearly impossible to sustain.

The Impact of NAFTA

Entering into force in 1994, the trade agreement allowed cheap foreign corn to flood the market, destabilizing the economy of small-scale producers in Guerrero.

The Magnet of Hops

The Yakima Valley is world-famous for producing over 75% of all hops in the United States. This crop requires highly meticulous and labor-intensive manual handling.

Workers from Guerrero quickly mastered these specialized techniques, turning agricultural towns like Toppenish and Granger into their new permanent homes.

Section 3

Culture, Beliefs, and Gastronomic Practices

The preservation of Guerrerense identity in Washington through living traditions.

Gastronomy as Cultural Resistance

Guerrerense food in the Yakima Valley is one of the most powerful expressions of cultural pride. Beyond mainstream Mexican commercial food, families prepare authentic southern dishes.

The traditional custom of eating Guerrerense Pozole (both white and green) on Thursdays, paired with mezcal, and preparing coastal mole, serve as essential social gathering points for this ethnic minority.

Section 4

Celebrations, Annual Events, and Prominent Members

Social cohesion, labor leadership, and shared community traditions.

Celebrations and Annual Events

The preservation of identity from Guerrero is heavily expressed through religious and civic annual festivities, serving as an invaluable cultural bridge for youth born in the U.S.

  • Patron Saint Festivals: Events dedicated to the saints of their home villages (such as the Virgin of Juquila or San Juan Bautista) featuring performances of the Danza de los Tecuanes (Jaguars) or Tlacololeros.
  • Day of the Dead: Community creation of traditional altars (ofrendas) adorned with marigolds, pan de yema, and regional southern dishes.

Leadership and Prominent Members

Agricultural Labor Leaders

Significant leadership emerges from grassroots organizing in independent labor unions like Familias Unidas por la Justicia (FUJ). Members of Mixtec and Triqui origin have fiercely advocated for fair wages, trilingual resources, and protections against extreme heat.

Local Entrepreneurs

Many families have successfully transitioned from seasonal field work to opening fixed, stable businesses. They have established authentic regional restaurants, grocery stores, and mechanic shops that revitalize cities like Toppenish, Sunnyside, and Yakima.

"Our festivities and our leaders prove that we are not an isolated community; we are an organized civic force that brings culture and labor rights to all of Washington."

Section 5

Employment Types Upon Immigration vs. Today

Analysis of socioeconomic mobility and labor diversification within the community.

Historical Evolution of Labor

Over the decades, the labor integration of Guerrerenses has shifted from strict reliance on seasonal agricultural jobs toward a strong presence in technical, service, and professional sectors.

Era / Context Dominant Sectors of Occupation Economic Characteristics
Initial Arrival (1980s - 1990s) Seasonal harvesting of apples, pears, and cherries. Heavy labor in hops fields. Dependence on seasonal agricultural cycles, minimum wage earnings, and high remittances sent to Guerrero.
Commercial Transition (2000s - 2010s) Orchard supervision, packing house jobs, construction, and roofing services. Reduced seasonal dependence. Residential stabilization and increase in homeownership.
Modern Era (Present Day) Healthcare services, bilingual education, logistics/transportation, and formal business ownership. Intergenerational mobility. Younger college-educated generations entering professional occupations.
